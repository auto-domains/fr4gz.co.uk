## Where the word came from

Long before it meant anything in a game, a **frag** was a fragmentation grenade, a weapon designed to throw out metal fragments when it explodes. During the Vietnam War, American soldiers used **fragging** to describe something much darker: deliberately attacking a fellow soldier, usually an unpopular officer or sergeant, with a grenade. The weapon left no fingerprints, and the word became part of military slang.

That is the meaning most dictionaries still list first. When gamers picked up the word two decades later, they kept the sound and dropped the seriousness.

## Doom and the first deathmatch

id Software released **Doom** in December 1993. As well as its single-player campaign, it let players link computers over a local network and hunt each other through its levels. id's John Romero is credited with naming this mode **deathmatch**.

In deathmatch, Doom's status bar swapped its weapons panel for a **frags** counter showing your kill count. It was a short, punchy word for a kill, it suited the game's military tone, and it spread quickly through the players swapping stories on bulletin boards, in university computer labs and at early LAN parties.

## Quake, clans and the internet

**Quake** arrived in 1996 and moved the action online. Its client and server design, improved for internet play by the **QuakeWorld** update later that year, meant players could frag strangers anywhere in the world instead of only the people on the same network.

Quake's community grew around that. Players formed **clans** with tags in front of their names, ran servers, built mods such as Team Fortress and Rocket Arena, and invented movement skills like **rocket jumping** and **strafe jumping** that the game's designers had not planned for. Leetspeak, the habit of swapping letters for numbers, was everywhere in clan names and nicknames, which is how a word like frags ends up written as **fr4gz**.

In 1997, the **Red Annihilation** Quake tournament, sponsored by Microsoft, ran online qualifiers before a live final. Dennis "Thresh" Fong won the final at E3 and took home John Carmack's Ferrari, a prize that made headlines and is often pointed to as an early moment for professional gaming.

## Arena shooters and the first pro leagues

**Quake III Arena** and **Unreal Tournament** both launched in 1999. They stripped away single-player stories and built everything around fast, skill-based fragging in purpose-made arenas. Unreal Tournament's announcer added its own vocabulary: double kill, multi kill, killing spree, rampage, dominating, unstoppable and godlike.

Organised competition grew alongside them. The **Cyberathlete Professional League**, founded in 1997, ran tournaments with cash prizes, and players began to be known by their nicknames well beyond their own servers.

## Counter-Strike changes the round

**Counter-Strike** began in 1999 as a Half-Life mod made by Minh "Gooseman" Le and Jess Cliffe, and Valve released it as a standalone game in 2000. It traded health pickups and respawns for rounds, money and one life each. Every frag mattered more, and new terms appeared to describe them: the **entry frag**, the **clutch**, the **ace** and the **eco round**.

Counter-Strike went on through Counter-Strike: Source, **Counter-Strike: Global Offensive** in 2012 and **Counter-Strike 2** in 2023. Its major tournaments have produced some of the most replayed frags in esports, a few of which you can watch on the [videos page](/videos/).

## The rise of the frag movie

Quake and Counter-Strike both recorded **demo files**, which store every movement in a match and let players watch it again from any angle. Players began cutting the best frags from their demos into videos set to music, and the **frag movie** was born.

Frag movies were shared on community sites and file servers long before video sites existed. The best ones were known for perfect timing between the action and the music, creative camera work and players whose aim seemed impossible. They set the template for the highlight reel, and many of their editors went on to work on esports broadcasts.

## Frags as a spectator sport

YouTube, launched in 2005, made frag movies easy to share, and live streaming, especially after Twitch launched in 2011, let fans watch players in real time. Tournament organisers began producing their own highlight videos within hours of a match.

The shooters that fill today's tournament schedules, such as **Rainbow Six Siege**, **Overwatch**, **Apex Legends** and **Valorant**, all share vocabulary that goes back to Doom and Quake. Players still talk about frags, fraggers and frag movies, and the scoreboard still counts kills. The word is more than thirty years old in gaming, and it shows no sign of being respawned as anything else.
